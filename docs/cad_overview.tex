{\large\color{muted}An overview of the pvs\_cad library and the \texttt{(cad)} proof strategy}\par\vspace{8pt}
{\large J.~Tanner Slagel{\renewcommand\thefootnote{*}\footnote{This report was written with Claude Code using Claude Opus 5.5. The pvs\_cad library was developed the same way, with Claude Fable 5.1 and then Opus 5 and Opus 5.5; every proof in it is checked by PVS. Claude Code: \url{https://claude.com/claude-code}.}}}\par\vspace{2pt}

\textbf{In short.} pvs\_cad is a PVS library, together with a proof strategy \texttt{(cad)}, that decides the
first-order theory of the real numbers: sentences built from polynomial equations and inequalities with rational
coefficients, the connectives AND, OR, NOT, IMPLIES and IFF, and quantifiers \texttt{FORALL}/\texttt{EXISTS} over real
variables, written in prenex form (every sentence has one). The procedure works in the manner of cylindrical algebraic decomposition (CAD), cell by cell over cylinders, and is written as an executable PVS function. The library
proves it \emph{sound} (an answer is always the truth) and \emph{complete} (it always answers). Inside a proof,
\texttt{(cad)} evaluates the procedure and turns the answer into a PVS proof that the kernel checks. The whole library,
233 theories and 3,506 formulas, is proved.

\section{What it looks like}
Real PVS sessions, with the simplifier's internal messages cut. \texttt{(cad)} decides one closed formula where it stands.
Four quantifiers, the quadratic formula as an existence theorem:
\begin{termbox}
\begin{Verbatim}[commandchars=\\«», fontsize=\footnotesize]
\Dm«d_quad_suf :»
\Dm«  |-------»
\Dm«{1}»   FORALL (a: real):
        FORALL (b: real):
          FORALL (c: real):
            EXISTS (x: real):
              a /= 0 AND b ^ 2 >= 4 * a * c IMPLIES
               a * x ^ 2 + b * x + c = 0
\Rl«Rule? (cad)»
\Ms«Deciding 1 by cylindrical decision, witness first,»
\Qd«Q.E.D.»
\Dm«Real time = 2.109 secs.»
\end{Verbatim}
\end{termbox}

Motzkin's polynomial~\cite{motzkin} is nonnegative, although it is not a sum of squares, so sum-of-squares certificates cannot prove it:
\begin{termbox}
\begin{Verbatim}[commandchars=\\«», fontsize=\footnotesize]
\Dm«b_motzkin :»
\Dm«  |-------»
\Dm«{1}»   FORALL (x: real):
        FORALL (y: real):
          x ^ 4 * y ^ 2 + x ^ 2 * y ^ 4 - 3 * x ^ 2 * y ^ 2 + 1 >= 0
\Rl«Rule? (cad)»
\Ms«Deciding 1 by cylindrical decision, witness first,»
\Qd«Q.E.D.»
\Dm«Real time = 1.135 secs.»
\end{Verbatim}
\end{termbox}

\texttt{(cad *)} works on a whole sequent. After \texttt{(skeep)} the facts sit in the sequent over skolem constants. The
strategy says which formulas it uses and which it skips, which terms become unknown reals, and which sentence it decides.
Here \texttt{member(x, l)} is not about reals and is skipped, while \texttt{f(x)} and \texttt{length(l)} become unknown
reals; that \texttt{length(l)} is nonnegative comes from its type:
\begin{termbox}
\begin{Verbatim}[commandchars=\\«», fontsize=\footnotesize]
\Dm«s_fun :»
\Dm«  |-------»
\Dm«{1}»   FORALL (x: real, l: list[real]):
        f(x) > x ^ 2 AND member(x, l) AND length(l) = 3 IMPLIES f(x) > -1
\Rl«Rule? (skeep)»
\Dm«{-1}»  f(x) > x ^ 2
\Dm«{-2}»  member(x, l)
\Dm«{-3}»  length(l) = 3
\Dm«  |-------»
\Dm«{1}»   f(x) > -1
\Rl«Rule? (cad *)»
\Ms«cad*: using (-1 -3 1), skipping (-2); unknown reals: cadv_1 = f(x), cadv_2 = x, cadv_3 = length(l)»
\Ms«cad*: deciding FORALL (cadv_1: real): FORALL (cadv_2: real): FORALL (cadv_3: real): (((cadv_3 >= 0)) IMPLIES (((cadv_1 > (cadv_2 ^ 2)) AND (cadv_3 = (3))) IMPLIES ((cadv_1 > (-1)))))»
\Ms«Deciding * by cylindrical decision, witness first,»
\Qd«Q.E.D.»
\Dm«Real time = 1.819 secs.»
\end{Verbatim}
\end{termbox}

A term that is not a polynomial: \texttt{sqrt(x)} becomes an unknown real, and the fact that it is nonnegative, which comes from its type, is added to the sentence. \texttt{(cad)} alone could not take this goal in any form:
\begin{termbox}
\begin{Verbatim}[commandchars=\\«», fontsize=\footnotesize]
\Dm«s_sqrt :»
\Dm«  |-------»
\Dm«{1}»   FORALL (x: nnreal, a: real):
        a = sqrt(x) AND a * a = x AND a > 1 IMPLIES x > 1
\Rl«Rule? (skeep)»
\Dm«{-1}»  a = sqrt(x)
\Dm«{-2}»  a * a = x
\Dm«{-3}»  a > 1
\Dm«  |-------»
\Dm«{1}»   x > 1
\Rl«Rule? (cad *)»
\Ms«cad*: using (-1 -2 -3 1), skipping nothing; unknown reals: cadv_1 = a, cadv_2 = sqrt(x), cadv_3 = x»
\Ms«cad*: deciding FORALL (cadv_1: real): FORALL (cadv_2: real): FORALL (cadv_3: real): (((cadv_2 >= 0) AND (cadv_3 >= 0)) IMPLIES (((cadv_1 = cadv_2) AND ((cadv_1 * cadv_1) = cadv_3) AND (cadv_1 > (1))) IMPLIES ((cadv_3 > (1)))))»
\Ms«Deciding * by cylindrical decision, witness first,»
\Qd«Q.E.D.»
\Dm«Real time = 2.602 secs.»
\end{Verbatim}
\end{termbox}

\section{How a proof by \texttt{(cad)} works}
The strategy follows the reflection template of Narkawicz, Muñoz and Dutle's univariate Sturm and Tarski strategies in NASALib~\cite{nasalib,nmd}. \texttt{(cad)} reads the goal and computes its encoding as data \texttt{(os, F, phi)}: the quantifier prefix, the polynomials' coefficient lists and the Boolean skeleton (see ``The main theorems''). It then runs
\texttt{decide5} in PVS's ground evaluator and instantiates \texttt{decide5\_correct} with that data. Unfolding
\texttt{fsem}, the semantics, turns the result back into the original formula: each quantifier is matched, and one reflection lemma per
polynomial (\texttt{peval\_pnorm}) shows that the coefficient list evaluates to the polynomial as written. A FALSE
sentence is reported and left in place, and a false hypothesis closes the goal. \texttt{(cad *)} extends this to a whole
sequent. It collects the polynomial hypotheses and goals, replaces non-polynomial real terms such as \texttt{sqrt(x)} or
\texttt{f(x)} by universally quantified reals, and decides the implication. The combined formula enters the proof by
\texttt{case}, so both branches are checked by PVS.

The trusted base is the PVS kernel plus the ground evaluator that runs \texttt{decide5}, the same base as NASALib's
Sturm and Tarski strategies; beyond that, one trusts the specification: that the semantics \texttt{fsem} and the
theorem statements (last section) say what is intended. The strategy code (1,957 lines of Lisp) is not trusted: it only chooses proof steps.

\section{What it decides in practice}
CAD is doubly exponential in the worst case, so completeness means ``always answers, given time''. Some measured
times for complete proofs (PVS's own timing, one M-series Mac):
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}X l r@{}}
\toprule
Sentence & Shape & Time\\
\midrule
Wilkinson's $(x-1)\cdots(x-20)$, expanded, is positive for $x<1$ (coefficients to $2.4\times10^{18}$) & $\forall$, degree 20 & 2.9\,s\\
Chebyshev $T_{10}$ stays in $[-1,1]$ on $[-1,1]$ & $\forall$, degree 10 & 2.0\,s\\
Motzkin's polynomial is nonnegative (not a sum of squares) & $\forall\forall$, degree 6 & 1.1\,s\\
Every depressed cubic $x^3+px+q$ has a real root & $\forall\forall\exists$ & 0.8\,s\\
Quadratic formula: $a\neq0 \wedge b^2\ge 4ac \Rightarrow \exists x\colon ax^2+bx+c=0$ & $\forall\forall\forall\exists$ & 2.1\,s\\
$\forall x\,\exists y\,\forall z\colon z^4+yz^2+y\ge x$ & $\forall\exists\forall$ & 22.9\,s\\
No real root of $z^2+bz+c$ implies $b^2<4c$, by \texttt{(cad *)} on a sequent & quantified hypothesis & 1.3\,s\\
\bottomrule

\end{center}
The limits show up with three or four variables at degree 3 to 4: AM--GM for three or four variables and
Cauchy--Schwarz in the plane do not finish in two minutes. On a set of 100 generated sentences with 1 to 6 quantifiers,
98 were proved within 300\,s, and no answer disagreed with Z3.

\section{How it was built}
The library was built in stages in September 2026 (275 commits). Most stages also shipped a verified strategy of their own.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.3em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Multivariate polynomials.} A normal form for multivariate polynomials over the rationals, proved unique, and
reflection lemmas connecting it to PVS real expressions (strategies \texttt{mpoly-eq}, \texttt{mpoly-simp}).
\item \textbf{Real algebraic numbers.} Root isolation, ordering and signs at algebraic numbers, on top of NASALib's
Sturm and Tarski libraries (\texttt{alg-roots}).
\item \textbf{Branching quantifier elimination.} One-variable quantifier elimination with parametric sign conditions
(\texttt{qe-exists}, \texttt{poly-sign}).
\item \textbf{Resultants and subresultants}: determinants, the resultant, and the subresultant theory behind
Collins's projection.
\item \textbf{A first cylindrical decision}, \texttt{decide}, proved correct for every sentence
(\texttt{decide\_correct}). It was complete but exponential in the number of polynomials: a circle and a line did not
finish in 15 minutes.
\item \textbf{The fast cylindrical decision.} This is the procedure \texttt{(cad)} runs. Over each cell of the lower variables, it lifts to
the next variable by walking exact rational separators between that variable's roots. It reads the sign vector at each
separator and at each root through Tarski queries at the cell's sample point, which may be algebraic. This builds one
tower of cells per outer sector, for any number of levels. Soundness
never assumes what it cannot check: the signs of the polynomials each lifting step reads are certified constant on each
sector at run time, which is proved to fix the set of sign vectors in every fibre over the sector, and a failed check sets
\texttt{ok} to FALSE (\texttt{decw\_correct} for two quantifiers, \texttt{decn\_correct} for any number).
\item \textbf{Completeness.} A review showed that the only way \texttt{ok} could be FALSE was a fixed search limit running
out. Some searches were made total; the others share one fuel parameter $u$. The proof shows that every large enough $u$
makes every check pass. Two
facts carry it. First, each search stops at its first success, so a larger $u$ changes nothing once $u$ suffices.
Second, the closures that add sign reads draw them from a finite set built level by level from \texttt{F}, so they reach
a fixpoint (\texttt{decn\_complete}). \texttt{decide5} raises $u$ until the checks pass; it terminates on the
non-computable measure ``least sufficient $u$'', which keeps it an ordinary executable PVS function
(\texttt{decn\_u\_complete}, then \texttt{decide5\_decides}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Where this sits}
Tarski showed that the first-order theory of the real numbers is decidable~\cite{tarski}, and Collins's
cylindrical algebraic decomposition~\cite{collins} made the decision practical; this library builds on the
PVS~\cite{pvs} libraries of NASALib~\cite{nasalib}, in particular the univariate Sturm and Tarski decision
procedures of Narkawicz, Mu\~noz and Dutle~\cite{nmd}. A formal correctness proof of CAD exists in
Rocq~\cite{vermande}, stated for an abstract real closed field; it is a correctness proof, not an executable
procedure used as a tactic. Isabelle/HOL has a verified, complete multivariate quantifier elimination
procedure~\cite{kosaian}, exported to executable code; its authors report that without virtual substitution
it ``appears to hang on all but the simplest univariate examples''. Lean's Mathlib~\cite{mathlib} has, as far
as we could find, no CAD formalization and no CAD-based decision tactic, and a Lean project's roadmap from
September 2026 lists CAD, delineability and Sturm theory among what Mathlib lacks~\cite{hex}; Lean's
nonlinear tactics (\texttt{nlinarith}, \texttt{polyrith}) are heuristic or check certificates found by an
external algebra system, and are not complete. To our knowledge (a survey of September 2026), pvs\_cad is
the first executable, proof-producing decision procedure of the CAD family that is formally verified sound and
complete and is usable as a tactic in an interactive prover. That its cells form a CAD in the classical sense
(connected, sign-invariant cells over continuous root functions) is not yet proved; that proof is in progress.

\section{The main theorems}
A sentence is encoded as data: \texttt{os} lists the quantifiers outermost first (\texttt{TRUE} for $\forall$,
\texttt{FALSE} for $\exists$), \texttt{F} lists the polynomials (exact rational coefficients), and \texttt{phi} is the
Boolean skeleton, whose atoms say ``polynomial $i$ has sign $s$''. Its meaning is defined by recursion on the prefix, with
the real quantifiers of PVS itself:

\begin{thmbox}{The semantics \Cm{\normalfont(cad\_decide.pvs)}}
\begin{Verbatim}
fsem(os, F, phi, ys): \Kw{RECURSIVE} bool =
  \Kw{CASES} os \Kw{OF}
    null: bfeval(phi, F, ys),
    cons(q, r): \Kw{IF} q \Kw{THEN} \Kw{FORALL} x: fsem(r, F, phi, cons(x, ys))
                \Kw{ELSE} \Kw{EXISTS} x: fsem(r, F, phi, cons(x, ys)) \Kw{ENDIF}
  \Kw{ENDCASES}
\end{Verbatim}
\end{thmbox}

The decision procedure \texttt{decide5} returns a record: \texttt{val}, its verdict, and \texttt{ok}, a flag that is
TRUE when its run-time checks passed. Three theorems say everything about it:

\begin{thmbox}{Soundness \Cm{\normalfont(cad\_decide5.pvs)}}
\begin{Verbatim}
decide5_correct: \Kw{THEOREM} decide5(reverse(os), F, phi)`ok \Kw{IMPLIES}
  (decide5(reverse(os), F, phi)`val \Kw{IFF} fsem(os, F, phi, null))
\end{Verbatim}
\end{thmbox}
\vspace{-4pt}
\begin{thmbox}{Completeness \Cm{\normalfont(complete\_all.pvs)}}
\begin{Verbatim}
decide5_complete: \Kw{THEOREM} decide5(reverse(os), F, phi)`ok
decide5_decides:  \Kw{THEOREM} decide5(reverse(os), F, phi)`val \Kw{IFF} fsem(os, F, phi, null)
\end{Verbatim}
\end{thmbox}

There are no hypotheses: \texttt{os}, \texttt{F} and \texttt{phi} are arbitrary, so the result covers every closed
prenex sentence with any number of quantifiers. \texttt{decide5} is a total function, and PVS generated and proved
its termination obligations. So the procedure always terminates, always passes its own checks, and always returns the
truth of the sentence.
